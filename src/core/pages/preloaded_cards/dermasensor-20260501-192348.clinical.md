# [DermaSensor](https://www.dermasensor.com/) — Clinical Model Card

## 1) Metadata
- **Model Name:** DermaSensor
- **Developer:** DermaSensor, Inc.
- **Regulatory Approval Status:** FDA Cleared
- **Regulatory Approval Date:** 2024-01-12
- **Version:** 3.0.2
- **Last Updated:** 2026-05-08
- **Availability:** US distribution to MD, FL, AZ, MS, PA, NY, CT, CA.

## 2) Summary
- **Summary:** DermaSensor is an AI-powered, software-aided adjunctive diagnostic device that uses optical spectroscopy, specifically Elastic Scattering Spectroscopy (ESS), and an artificial intelligence/machine learning (AI/ML) algorithm to non-invasively analyze intact skin lesions. It is indicated for use by physicians who are not dermatologists to evaluate skin lesions suggestive of melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, and/or squamous cell carcinoma in patients aged 40 and above, assisting in the decision regarding referral to a dermatologist. The device provides results as "Monitor" (negative) or "Investigate Further" (positive) with an additional Similarity Score of 1-10 for positive outputs.

## 3) Intended Use
- **Medical Purpose:** The DermaSensor device is indicated for use to evaluate skin lesions suggestive of melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, and/or squamous cell carcinoma in patients aged 40 and above. Its primary purpose is to assist physicians who are not dermatologists in the decision regarding referral of the patient to a dermatologist.
- **Acceptable Input:** The device takes input from an intact skin lesion by non-invasively applying a handheld unit to its surface. These light samples are then conveyed to a microspectrometer, yielding Elastic Scattering Spectroscopy (ESS) spectral recordings. The device tip contacts intact skin for less than 3 minutes, and the user remains gloved during operation.
- **Output:** The results are provided to the user at the point of care as either "Monitor," for a negative result, or "Investigate Further," for a positive result. For a positive output ("Investigate Further"), the device also displays a "Similarity Score" ranging from 1 to 10, where higher scores indicate greater similarity to signals observed in malignant lesions.
- **Role in Decision-Making:** Provides adjunctive referral support for physicians evaluating suspicious lesions; does not replace clinician judgment, biopsy, or definitive diagnosis.
- **Intended Users:** Physicians who are not dermatologists. Users must be physicians, though not necessarily dermatologists, and are expected to perform a clinical assessment, including visual analysis of the lesion, prior to using the device. The device is a prescription device, restricting its sale, distribution, and use in accordance with 21 CFR 801.109.
- **Target Patient Population:** Patients aged 40 and above with skin lesions suggestive of melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, and/or squamous cell carcinoma. The device is intended for use on primary lesions only. The training and tuning data for the algorithm included a majority of older patients (age 60-79), Fitzpatrick phototype I-III, and individuals who self-identified as White, reflecting the higher incidence of skin cancer in these populations.

## 4) Risks and Limitations
- **Known Performance Limitations:** The performance of the device has not been specifically evaluated in patients with increased risk for skin cancer (e.g., inherited or drug-induced photosensitivity, genetic predisposition to melanoma or BCC, immune compromise, or other medical conditions that increase the risk of skin cancer or its metastasis). It is intended to assist in clinical decisions related only to melanoma (including severely atypical nevi), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and BCC, and has not been tested on rare skin cancer types; therefore, it should not be used for lesions suggestive of malignancies other than these three. The device is intended for use on primary lesions only and has not been tested on lesions that are previously biopsied, recurrent, or metastatic; on scars, tattoos, sunburned skin, or within a hairy area; or which are located on palms, soles, mucosal surfaces, genitals, ears, within 1 cm of the eye, or under nails. Labeling must include warnings to avoid unsafe exposure to any energy-emitting components, e.g., excluding lesions close to the eye. Not for screening or unsupervised population triage. Not a standalone diagnostic device. Not the sole basis for diagnosis or referral decisions. Does not confirm a clinical diagnosis of cancer. Does not replace biopsy, pathology review, or clinician decision-making.
- **Skin Type Representation & Equity:** Consistent with the lower prevalence of skin cancer in Fitzpatrick skin phototypes IV-VI, less data is available for sensitivity of the device for melanoma in these patients, and the decision to refer patients with suspicious pigmented lesions in this group should be primarily based on clinical concern.
- **Required Clinician Oversight:** A prescribing physician (not necessarily a dermatologist) must operate the device and interpret its output. Independent clinical assessment, including visual lesion evaluation, is required before and alongside device use. Referral decisions remain with the clinician; device output provides adjunctive support only. Definitive diagnosis requires clinician judgment and biopsy or pathology confirmation where clinically indicated.

## 5) Mechanism and Development
- **Model Type:** AI/ML classifier analyzing Elastic Scattering Spectroscopy (ESS) recordings from intact skin lesions.
- **Training Data:** The training dataset contained 950 lesions, contributing approximately 4,200 Elastic Scattering Spectroscopy (ESS) spectra. The algorithm was trained in the spectral range of 360 to 810 nm, using 47 selected wavelengths to reduce dimensionality. Patient demographics for the training dataset were: Age (20-39: 7.6%, 40-59: 8.2%, 60-79: 56.6%, 80+: 4.2%, Unknown: 23.4%); Fitzpatrick Phototype (I: 6.3%, II: 31.1%, III: 22.9%, IV: 4.3%, V: 0.7%, VI: 0.3%, Unknown: 34.4%); Ethnicity/Race (White: 73.5%, Black or African American: 0.5%, Asian: 0.8%, Multiracial: 0.2%, Native Hawaiian or Other: 1.6%, Unknown: 23.4%); Gender (Male: 39.7%, Female: 24.8%, Unknown: 35.4%). Anatomical locations included sun-exposed areas like head/neck (5.9%), chest/upper back (3.3%), forearms/hands (3.5%), lower legs (1.4%), and unexposed areas like buttocks/hips (0.1%), upper legs (1.1%), lower trunk (0.7%). The diagnoses included melanoma and severely dysplastic nevi (8.2%), SCC (9.0%), BCC (12.8%), mixed malignant (0.1%), and various benign mimics such as SK (brown) (6.9%), Benign Nevi (27.5%), AK (7.5%), SK (nonbrown) (3.3%), and other benign/malignant lesions.
- **Model Development & Validation:** The algorithm was developed and tested using a dataset that included 950 lesions (contributing ~4,200 spectra) for training and 361 independent, randomly chosen lesions for testing, totaling 1,311 skin lesions. The training and tuning data predominantly included older patients (age 60-79), Fitzpatrick phototypes I-III, and individuals who self-identified as White, reflecting the higher incidence of skin cancer in these populations. Lesions on palms, soles, and under nails were excluded. The anatomical locations were primarily head and neck, followed by chest/upper back and forearms/hands. The diagnoses included BCC, SCC, melanoma (and severely atypical nevi), and various benign mimics. For postmarket surveillance, FDA requires collection and reporting of data from clinical validation performance testing in patients from demographic groups representative of the U.S. population, specifically to include populations with limited representation in premarket studies (e.g., Fitzpatrick skin phototypes IV, V, and VI) or where sensitivity was below 90%. This postmarket study requires a statistically justified sample size to confirm non-inferior performance, with overrepresentation of Fitzpatrick skin phototypes with lower prevalence of skin cancer, and recording of age, sex, socioeconomic status, race, and risk factors for skin cancer development and progression. [Publication](One peer-reviewed publication is referenced: [5] *   Rodriguez-Diaz et al. [5] Photochem, Photobiol. [5] 2019;95(6):1441-1445. [5])
- **Clinical Trial:** Clinical development for the DermaSensor device included a blinded, multicenter clinical study through an initial ML retrospective algorithm, followed by a subsequent blinded, multicenter clinical study. These studies investigated the performance of an algorithm using Elastic Scattering Spectroscopy (ESS) and Machine Learning (ML) for common types of skin cancers. The initial study dataset was later used entirely for algorithm training, comprising 950 lesions for training and 361 lesions for testing, totaling 1,311 skin lesions. The training and testing datasets were independent and chosen randomly. As part of the FDA De Novo classification, the developer is required to conduct postmarket clinical validation performance testing in patients from demographic groups representative of the U.S. population, including populations with limited representation of melanomas in premarket studies (e.g., Fitzpatrick skin phototypes IV, V, and VI) or where sensitivity was lower than 90%. This postmarket study must include a statistically justified sample size to confirm device performance, enroll a representative range of subjects with overrepresentation of Fitzpatrick skin phototypes IV, V, and VI, and record age, sex, socioeconomic status, race, and skin cancer risk factors to assess their impact on the device’s effectiveness. The study protocol was required to be submitted within 30 days of the January 12, 2024 order, with specific enrollment milestones: first subject enrolled within 6 months, 20% within 12 months, 50% within 24 months, and 100% within 36 months from protocol approval. Periodic progress reports are also required.
- **Performance:** Sensitivity 96.5% (n=221/229); Specificity 20.6% (n=28/136); Specificity 33.0% (n=128/388).
- **Performance — Sensitivity:** 96.5% (n=221/229)
- **Performance — Specificity:** 20.6% (n=28/136)
- **Performance — Specificity:** 33.0% (n=128/388)
- **Limitations in Evaluating Performance:** The patient demographics for the algorithm training and tuning datasets included Fitzpatrick phototypes I (6.3% training, 14.6% tuning), II (31.1% training, 36.5% tuning), III (22.9% training, 20.8% tuning), IV (4.3% training, 11.4% tuning), V (0.7% training, 12.3% tuning), and VI (0.3% training, 3.7% tuning), with a significant portion unknown (34.4% training, 0.7% tuning). The FDA noted that less data was available for sensitivity of the DermaSensor device for melanoma in Fitzpatrick skin phototypes IV-VI, consistent with the lower prevalence of skin cancer in these groups. Due to this, the FDA requires postmarket clinical validation performance testing to include populations who had limited representation of melanomas in the premarket studies (e.g., Fitzpatrick skin phototypes IV, V, and VI) or in whom sensitivity was lower than 90%, with an overrepresentation of these skin phototypes in the study enrollment.

## 6) Governance and Monitoring
- **Plans for Ongoing Monitoring:** The DermaSensor device has a minimum use life of three years, with product use-life testing performed to ensure adequacy. Software verification, validation, and hazard analysis are performed as part of special controls. The cybersecurity documentation includes an upgrade and maintenance plan. There was a Class 2 Device Recall (Z-0583-2026) initiated by the firm on October 13, 2025 (posted November 21, 2025) for DermaSensor (Model/Catalog Number: 10101, Software Version: 3.0.2) due to a potential for the device to not meet specifications, resulting in incorrect results or delayed patient referral. The recall action involved notifying consignees via telephone and follow-up letter, instructing them to examine inventory, complete a response form, and arrange a visit with DermaSensor for device correction. If a device contained the component issue, DermaSensor would provide correct results for past scans.
