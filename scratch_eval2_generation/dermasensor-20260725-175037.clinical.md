# [DermaSensor](https://www.dermasensor.com/) — Clinical Model Card

## 1) Metadata
- **Model Name:** DermaSensor [1]
- **Developer:** DermaSensor [1]
- **Regulatory Approval Status:** Regulatory Status Unknown
- **Regulatory Approval Date:** Not disclosed
- **Version:** Algorithm version 3.0 (used for reanalysis of study results) [1]
- **Last Updated:** 2026-07-25
- **Availability:** The device is commercially available in the U.S. following FDA De Novo clearance in early 2024. An introductory offer is available, and demos can be requested.

## 2) Summary
- **Summary:** The DermaSensor device uses optical spectroscopy and an AI/ML algorithm to non-invasively analyze intact skin lesions. It provides a classification of "Monitor" or "Investigate Further" to assist non-dermatologist physicians in deciding on referral for lesions suspicious for melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, and squamous cell carcinoma in patients aged 40 and above. It is not a standalone diagnostic or screening tool. [1]

## 3) Intended Use
- **Medical Purpose:** To evaluate skin lesions suggestive of melanoma, basal cell carcinoma (BCC), and/or squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) in patients aged 40 and above to assist physicians who are not dermatologists in the decision regarding referral to a dermatologist. [1]
- **Acceptable Input:** Optical spectroscopy (Elastic Scattering Spectroscopy, ESS) spectral recordings of intact skin lesions, obtained by non-invasively applying the handheld device's fiber-optic probe tip to the lesion surface. The device transmits broadband white light (360 to 810 nm) and collects backscattered light samples. [1]
- **Output:** A classification of "Monitor" (negative result) or "Investigate Further" (positive result) generated by a proprietary ML-derived classifier algorithm analyzing optical recordings of backscattered light. For positive results, a Similarity Score of 1-10 is displayed, indicating resemblance to malignant lesions, but the device does not differentiate among malignant pathologies. [1]
- **Role in Decision-Making:** The device is intended for use as an adjunctive second-read device following identification of a suspicious skin lesion by a physician not trained in the clinical diagnosis and management of skin cancer. It assists in clinical decisions regarding patient referral to a dermatologist and should be used in conjunction with the totality of clinically relevant information from the clinical assessment. It is not for use as a standalone diagnostic, screening tool, or to confirm a clinical diagnosis. [1]
- **Intended Users:** Physicians who are not dermatologists, such as primary care physicians (PCPs). The device is a prescription device. [1]
- **Target Patient Population:** Patients aged 40 and above with primary skin lesions suggestive of melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, and/or squamous cell carcinoma. [1]

## 4) Risks and Limitations
- **Known Performance Limitations:** Performance has not been specifically evaluated in patients with increased risk for skin cancer (e.g., inherited or drug-induced photosensitivity, genetic predisposition, immune compromise). It has not been tested on rare skin cancer types. The device is for primary lesions only and not for previously biopsied, recurrent, or metastatic lesions; on scars, tattoos, sunburned skin, hairy areas; or on palms, soles, mucosal surfaces, genitals, ears, within 1 cm of the eye, or under nails. Less data is available for sensitivity of melanoma in Fitzpatrick skin phototypes IV-VI. [1]
- **Skin Type Representation & Equity:** Less data is available for sensitivity of the DermaSensor device for melanoma in Fitzpatrick skin phototypes IV-VI. For these patients, the decision to refer suspicious pigmented lesions should be primarily based on clinical concern. FDA requires postmarket clinical validation performance testing in demographic groups representative of the U.S. population, including populations with limited representation of melanomas in premarket studies (e.g., Fitzpatrick skin phototypes IV, V, and VI). [1]
- **Required Clinician Oversight:** Yes, the device is a prescription device and requires clinician oversight. It must be used by physicians not trained in dermatology, in conjunction with their clinical assessment and visual analysis of the lesion. It is not a standalone diagnostic and does not replace clinical decision-making. [1]

## 5) Mechanism and Development
- **Model Type:** Artificial intelligence/machine learning (AI/ML) based software algorithm utilizing Elastic Scattering Spectroscopy (ESS). The final architecture employs 10 neural networks, including feedforward and deep neural networks based on ResNet-18 architecture. [1]
- **Training Data:** The AI algorithm was trained using an expansive databank of spectral scans from over 10,000 readings from over 2,000 lesions, collected from various company-sponsored and independent investigator clinical studies. The training and tuning datasets primarily included older patients (60-79 years), Fitzpatrick phototypes I-III, and individuals who self-identified as White. Lesions on the head/neck constituted the majority. The dataset included BCC, SCC, melanoma (including highly atypical nevi), and various benign mimics.
- **Model Development & Validation:** The algorithm was developed and tested on datasets to differentiate malignant from benign melanocytic lesions and later for common skin cancer types (melanoma, SCC, BCC). The algorithm was trained in the spectral range of 360 to 810 nm, using 47 selected wavelengths. The final architecture has 10 neural networks, including feedforward and deep neural networks based on ResNet-18. The algorithm version used in the initial study was 1.0, later revised to 3.0 for reanalysis of study results. The study received IRB approval from the Western Institution Review Board (Protocol CSP-18-0001). [Publication](https://www.bu.edu/bmo/files/2024/02/Dermasensor-paper-2.pdf)
- **Clinical Trial:** NCT05126173 — [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT05126173) — The DERM-SUCCESS study was a prospective, multicenter clinical validation study led by the Mayo Clinic across 22 study centers, enrolling over 1000 patients. It assessed the safety and effectiveness of the ESS device in evaluating lesions suggestive of skin cancer. A companion clinical utility study also investigated DermaSensor’s usage with 108 physicians.
- **Performance:** In the DERM-SUCCESS study, the DermaSensor device demonstrated an overall sensitivity of 96% across all 224 types of skin cancers. Negative results from DermaSensor had a 97% chance of being benign across all skin cancers. A separate comparative effectiveness study found an overall device sensitivity of 97.04% and overall specificity of 26.22%. No statistically significant difference was found between the device and dermatologist sensitivity (P = .8203). A clinical utility study showed the device decreased missed skin cancers by half (18% vs 9%) and increased clinician referrals (81% vs 94%).
- **Performance — Overall Sensitivity:** 97.04% (All lesions suggestive of skin cancer)
- **Performance — Melanoma Sensitivity:** 96.67% (Melanoma (including severely atypical nevi))
- **Performance — Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC) Sensitivity:** 97.22% (Basal Cell Carcinoma)
- **Performance — Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC) Sensitivity:** 97.01% (Squamous Cell Carcinoma)
- **Performance — Overall Specificity:** 26.22% (All lesions suggestive of skin cancer)
- **Performance — Negative Predictive Value (NPV):** 89.58% (All lesions suggestive of skin cancer)
- **Performance — Positive Predictive Value (PPV):** 57.54% (All lesions suggestive of skin cancer)
- **Limitations in Evaluating Performance:** The overall specificity of the device was 26.22% compared to dermatologists' 56.10%, assuming all unbiopsied lesions enrolled by dermatologists were benign. The study population for training and tuning was predominantly older patients (age 60-79), Fitzpatrick phototype I-III, and self-identified White individuals, reflecting higher incidence of skin cancer in these groups, but also indicating potential limitations in other populations. [1]

## 6) Governance and Monitoring
- **Plans for Ongoing Monitoring:** FDA requires postmarket clinical validation performance testing to collect and report surveillance data. This testing must demonstrate the device performs as intended in the intended patient population, specifically including demographic groups representative of the U.S. population, such as Fitzpatrick skin phototypes IV, V, and VI, or populations where sensitivity was lower than 90%. A study protocol is required within 30 days of the FDA order, with subject enrollment milestones (first subject within 6 months, 100% within 36 months) and annual progress reports.
- **Scheduled Updates:** The DermaSensor Coding Guide was last updated on July 22, 2026. No explicit schedule for device or algorithm updates is provided in the sources, beyond the post-market surveillance requirements for clinical performance validation and ongoing reporting.
